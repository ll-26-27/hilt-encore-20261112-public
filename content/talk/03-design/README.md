---
title: How we designed it, and how the design shifted
question: What happens when the tool changes faster than the course?
who: Madeleine, then Marlon
minutes: 15
---

The plan was four days: take faculty from wherever they were, even with no coding or command-line experience, to using Claude Code. The first time through anything, you're designing it as much as delivering it. And the plan morphed every day, because the tools changed constantly.

We wanted to give people an overview of all the things it does well. The first demos we'd seen on campus had Claude reorganize someone's Downloads folder: incredibly powerful, not amazing for risk or security. So we built the recipe-card activity instead. Photos of old recipe cards go in. Claude reads the text on the cards, renames each file from what's inside it, researches the history of each recipe, and builds an interactive web gallery: the card, the recipe, and a short essay on it. In miniature, it shows the maneuvers of academic work: analyzing visual data, getting text from images, contextualizing research, and producing reports and galleries for teaching.

Its point of failure was informative. People finished the whole exercise, and many still couldn't see how to move from it to something useful for them. So we extended the metaphor: what you make with Claude is a recipe, ingredients and the things you do with them to get a finished dish. We printed large recipe cards and invited faculty to give us their use cases, and we built recipe repositories they could open to see how we'd set up Claude Code for a piece of their kind of knowledge work.

Meanwhile the ground moved. We started out thinking we couldn't teach the desktop app because it was useless; by the end it was the only way we taught. For a while the app had three tabs, Chat, Cowork, and Code, and we built our handouts around that tripartite structure; then the app changed and we had to throw it out. Our multiplication activity, which for a year gave people deep intuitions about where the models go wrong, stopped failing once the models started calling tools. Anthropic uses AI to build the app, so it changes faster than ever; we use the app to build our materials faster than ever. An arms race between the teachers and the territory they're teaching.

And the hardest problems were procedural. Copying a path. Understanding that the nesting of folders you click through is a path the computer can read. Even in disciplines where we assumed people lived in the command line, they didn't necessarily.

We also taught with AI ourselves: every day we took the transcript of the session and asked what mistakes we'd made, and rehearsed the explanations we'd gotten wrong.
