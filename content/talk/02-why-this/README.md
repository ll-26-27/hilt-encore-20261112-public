title: Why this particular AI thing?
question: Why a four-day intensive on Claude Code?
who: Marlon, then Madeleine

We've trained faculty for a while. Early on that meant department visits: one piece of information given to everyone at once, as if they were all in the same place, with very different opinions about AI. There are moments when that's valuable and necessary, but in an hour, pitched to people brand new to it, you never get past the first hour, and the first hour doesn't get people to the point where they can have meaningful interactions with the tools.

Office hours were another way in. Madeleine tells that part: how we were trying to figure out how to get our ideas out to faculty.

Meanwhile, the question of quality changed. For a while many people assumed the penalty for using ChatGPT was that you just wouldn't get a good grade, because the work would be full of flaws and errors. For some disciplines at least, that's no longer the case. In the FAS Current on October 29, Harvard's director of graduate studies in Mathematics, Melanie Matchett Wood, put it this way: "AI can produce things that could have been acceptable Ph.D. theses a couple of years ago." A Harvard-led summit of mathematicians concluded that written work by itself, "even a brilliant dissertation", is no longer sufficient proof of mastery. But getting to that level takes skill with the tool, time with the tool, and some awareness of how to steer it; leveraging expertise yields better outcomes than naive one-shot prompting.

So we wanted a longer, more intensive training: not the only thing we'd do, but one that would bring faculty to the point where they use AI the way we do in our own workflows. Claude Code has been a game changer for everyone we've seen use it. We designed a four-day intensive to take faculty from wherever they were, even with no coding or command-line experience, to using Claude Code. It overbooked so quickly that we added two more.

And the question is everywhere. On November 11, the Guardian reported that England's university regulator, the Office for Students, says AI use "presents a generational risk" and wants "far greater use of AI-free assessment"; one staff member told its researchers that AI "may render our assessment of students close to impossible in some areas."
