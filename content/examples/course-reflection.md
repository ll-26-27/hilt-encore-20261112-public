## What this is

We taught the same four-day workshop three times. The syllabus stayed recognizable; the experience kept changing. The calendar from our slides becomes something you can explore here: switch weeks, compare the plan with the record, and open a day to see what moved.

## How we made it

We use AI to help us read across more material than we can hold in memory at once: session transcriptions, handwritten planning notes, revisions and folders of teaching materials. It can line up a promised activity with what we actually spent time explaining, follow an exercise across several weeks, and bring a small remark back into view. That helps us reflect together instead of rebuilding the story from whichever moments we remember best.

This calendar uses the same twelve-session reconstruction as the slides. Its observed layer is anchored to eleven transcripts; retained plans and written reflections supply the other layers. Handwritten notes are part of our broader reflection workflow, not a claim that every calendar block was extracted from a scan. We keep those distinctions visible: a plan is not evidence that an activity happened, and a later explanation is not a contemporaneous observation.

The pattern gives us questions rather than a score. Did setup crowd out practice? When did context-file exercises become concrete? Did a task recipe help faculty connect the tools to their own work? The final day, June 11, has no central transcript, so it stays visibly unverified. The blocks are ordered activities, not measured durations.
